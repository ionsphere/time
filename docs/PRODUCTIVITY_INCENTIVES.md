# TIME — Why Would Anyone Become More Productive?
## Incentives, work obligations, voluntary effort, and the automation paradox · v0.3

> **Foundational question:** If a fisherman earns one time credit per minute actually worked, why would he catch 100 fish/hour instead of 10? Neither an invariant production-cost unit nor interest-free money guarantees that individuals will seek progress.

This is the most important adversarial test for TIME. **Labor spent, output produced, and reward earned are three different variables.** The simulator must *never* silently set them equal.

This document extends [Household Economy](HOUSEHOLD_ECONOMY.md) and [Economic Model](ECONOMIC_MODEL.md). It does not replace the idea that fish are paid for with credits transferred from buyers.

## 1. The actual contradiction

Assume 10 fish are needed and the only variable production cost is fishing labor:

| | Old method | Improved method |
|---|---:|---:|
| Productivity | 10 fish/h | 100 fish/h |
| Time spent fulfilling 10-fish order | 60 min | 6 min |
| Nominal credits for actual labor under strict hourly pay | 60 TC | 6 TC |
| Calendar time saved | 0 min | 54 min |

If the fisherman still needs housing and services whose labor cost has *not* declined, 54 saved minutes **do not compensate** for losing 54 transferable credits. This can make improving productivity privately irrational even when it is socially valuable.

Conversely, paying 60 TC for six minutes of work without changing any other rule can keep fish prices artificially high. Paying per fish without demand or ecological checks rewards waste and overfishing.

**There is no physical-law solution to this distribution problem.** The game must compare explicit institutions that determine who benefits from new productive capacity.

## 2. A fundamental alternative: finish an accepted obligation, then be free

Replace “work for eight hours to earn eight hours” with “fulfill an agreed deliverable obligation to secure your consumption entitlement.”

A neighborhood needs 100 fish per day. Initially ten people each take an hour to supply ten fish. With better equipment, a single fisherman can supply 100 in an hour. The community's required fishing labor falls from 10 person-hours to one. Nine hours of obligatory fishing disappear.

An individual who commits to ten fish can fulfill the commitment in six minutes instead of an hour. **The direct, non-market reward is 54 minutes of freedom.** They may choose more work, cooking, art, education, care, or nothing.

This only works as an incentive if the fisher's access to necessities does **not** fall proportionally with the decline in required labor. A pure “credits = minutes actually worked” rule fails this condition.

### Distinguish two planes

- **Production cost:** the real, verifiable reference labor/time attributable to ten fish falls from 60 to 6 fishing minutes. Upstream fishing-related costs may also change, but are counted separately. Prices of newly produced fish reflect the new efficient production cost; the physical unit of time stays immutable.
- **Access / claim distribution:** community members' ability to obtain fish, housing, care and other goods cannot be defined solely by current minutes of obligatory work in an increasingly automated economy. Some contributions are recognized by commitments; some essential access is collectively funded or rights-based. Any monetary transfers are explicit and financed, not conjured by merely saving time.

A quota is **not** a fixed number of hours to burn. It is an agreed, quality-checked outcome. Unwanted additional fish do not qualify, and fishing must respect sustainable stock constraints.

## 3. What if he could catch 100, but people only want ten?

Then he should catch **ten and go home**. Producing 90 fish nobody needs or which damage the fish population is not a better economic outcome.

The objective of an economy is not maximum catch-per-hour at all times. It is **meeting the needs and chosen wants of people with minimal necessary burdens and within physical/ecological constraints**.

What if people need 100? The fisherman can deliver them in an hour, releasing up to nine *other* fishermen-hours of required labor versus the old method. The community must decide how to distribute the benefits fairly: reduce everyone's necessary work, redirect some people to unmet tasks, or support a transition. “The nine other fishers have lost their wage income” is a legitimate failure mode, not proof of prosperity.

## 4. Candidate non-market incentive constitutions

The first playable should let the player compare these regimes with identical people, fish supply, physical technology, and demand.

### Regime A — Strict elapsed-time pay (control case)

Workers earn one TC per verified minute of accepted work. Every new product's certified labor cost declines with efficient methods. Voluntary producers can sell at cost. No interest.

**Predicted failure risk:** a worker's earnings may shrink even if others benefit; people may deliberately avoid labor-saving tools, seek unnecessary work, or overproduce. If money is required for every necessity, technological progress can be individually punishing.

### Regime B — Accepted-output obligations + stable basic access (primary candidate)

Households have transparent **access to a defined baseline of necessities**, funded by shared productive contributions, agreed future obligations, or community commons capacity. Able participants accept an appropriate share of **outcome commitments**, not a mandated number of on-the-clock minutes. The right to ordinary participation is not withdrawn just because a person learned to finish tasks efficiently.

For optional or above-baseline goods, people exchange TC earned through accepted contributions and settled obligations. This retains **“someone else's fish is paid for”** while separating survival from compulsory make-work.

**Reward:** completing work early creates real discretionary time; helping others eliminate required tasks may grant community standing, right to choose future projects, and optional recognition. This isn't a disguised monetary wage, and citizens with different motivations may react differently.

**Hard questions:** Who sets the fair share of commitments? How are care, disability, lifelong training, health, and family responsibilities recognized? Who has a claim to scarce specialist time or prime housing sites? Can this system function when productivity improvements remove most accepted human tasks?

### Regime C — Finite, rule-based productivity dividend (optional supplement)

An inventor or early process improver may receive a bounded share of **verified incremental required labor avoided**, not an indefinite property claim or interest. It is paid **from participating consumers/communities' existing credits or an explicitly funded commons account**, not minted from saved hours.

For 10 fish:
- Old direct fishing effort: 60 min; new direct effort: 6 min; **54 min avoided**.
- Suppose upstream allocated effort stays 30 min/batch (illustrative constant, not assumed universal). New **production cost** is 36 TC, down from 90 TC.
- If a time-limited public productivity prize pays **20% of direct avoided work**, the fisher/inventor receives 10.8 TC **from a funded innovation pool**, as a separate payment. The fish itself remains priced at its audited production cost of 36 TC.
- Buyers save 54 TC on that batch; society's aggregate net transfer to the inventor is 10.8 TC, leaving 43.2 TC of benefit distributed elsewhere, subject to how the innovation pool is financed.
- If the inventor is not the fisherman, direct the prize to the actual inventor, rather than giving a fisher ownership of someone else's idea. If fishing productivity improves only by the fisher's greater skill or creative adaptation, the policy may recognize a verified local process improvement instead.

No innovation reward is due for catching unneeded fish, violating ecosystem limits, or degrading quality. Set a finite cap and sunset; after that, the lower reference cost remains.

**Risk:** the saved-effort comparison depends on an explicit counterfactual, and rewards still require someone else to fund them. This cannot be claimed as a uniquely derived law of nature. It is a constitutional redistribution rule, openly tested.

### Regime D — Pure communal recognition / intrinsic motivation (laboratory case)

No private productivity dividend. Faster workers get earlier release from accepted obligations; inventors may gain public recognition, research latitude, friendships, mastery and creative satisfaction. Citizens with heterogeneous intrinsic motivation determine how much innovation actually occurs.

**Predicted failure risk:** some people improve for personal freedom or curiosity; others have no reason to invest large research effort for benefits they cannot access. This regime may underinvest in uncertain innovation unless research itself is a paid accepted public service.

## 5. The “ratchet” problem

If the fisherman becomes 10× faster, and society immediately increases his personal required output tenfold while preserving his working hours, **the fisherman experiences no freedom dividend**. That removes one of the strongest non-market incentives.

A viable accepted-obligation system must define who retains efficiency gains and for how long:
- Does the fisherman keep their earlier deliverable target for a fair transition period, allowing early release?
- If fish needs remain constant, do all contributors receive some reduction in obligatory work?
- How are now-redundant fishers supported and retrained without demanding pointless fishing?
- Does a process discoverer receive a finite bonus without charging forever?
- How do contributions adapt as society as a whole requires less work?

A deliberately hostile game policy automatically increases each fisher's quota whenever they innovate. Compare it against shared reductions in obligatory work and measure adoption of better methods.

## 6. Agent motivations must be plural and observable

No one can be assumed to be intrinsically charitable or to maximize credit balances. For each agent, include variable:
- Desire for discretionary time, family time, health, leisure.
- Security of access to food, housing, medicine, transportation and care.
- Satisfaction with skilled craft, efficiency, mastery, curiosity.
- Social reciprocity, reputation, esteem and civic relationships.
- Ambition for optional comforts and higher-quality experiences.
- Fairness preferences: resentment when efficiency is punished, work is dumped on others, or contributions aren't recognized.
- Ecological concern and aversion to waste.

For each decision, log **why** the agent chose it, plus work time, fish output, quality, ecological cost, earned TC, household access, and actual next use of recovered time.

## 7. Specific game test: The Better Fisher

Two comparable neighborhoods, identical initial people, preferences, lake stocks and physical needs. Introduce the technique that allows 100 fish/hour instead of 10.

Test choices:
1. Ten fish ordered, no added demand. Does the fisherman stop after six minutes or fish for a full hour? Why?
2. 100 fish ordered. Do communities reassign/release nine workers rather than waste labor?
3. The faster method reduces the fishing wage from 60 to six under strict elapsed-time pay. Does the fisher adopt it or sabotage its disclosure?
4. Can a child household and an ill fisher access necessities when they provide few accepted productive minutes?
5. If commitments are automatically ratcheted, does invention stop?
6. If a bounded innovation reward exists, is it paid without minting credits or misallocating upstream work?
7. What if greater catches deplete breeding stock and harm future food supply?
8. If some agents care deeply about excellence while others prefer leisure, how different is invention adoption and community well-being?
9. If the faster method is open-source, can multiple fishers adopt it without duplicate royalty claims and still lower fish costs?
10. If the community only needs a few fish, does the optimal solution deliberately leave unused productive capacity idle?

Metrics: calories delivered per person, unmet demand, fish stock, ecological sustainability, labor required per essential, paid work availability, credits earned/spent, discretionary time, household security, inequality, voluntary research effort, invention adoption, queue length, total accepted obligations.

**Success is not universally more fish**. It's fewer required hours to sustain an adequate and chosen standard of living, without excluding those who cannot work or impoverishing those who improve methods.

## 8. Game-design consequence

**The first interactive mystery after buying fish should be:**
> “You can now catch 100 fish an hour. What would make you want to use the new method—and who gets the nine hours of life it saves?”

Players can choose a constitutional rule and then **watch individual fishermen and their families** change their behavior.

No physics dictates the right distribution. That is what the simulation should make intelligible.
